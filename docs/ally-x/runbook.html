Tuning the ROG Ally X for handheld and docked play

Eleven phases, in order, for an ASUS ROG Ally X (2024) running Windows 11 with Steam. Software, settings, and external accessories only — nothing here opens the chassis or touches your warranty.

Where the gains actually are

1

Render resolution and frame caps (Phase 7). Larger than every registry tweak combined. 900p + FSR Quality at a 40 fps cap beats native 1080p uncapped on every axis that matters — smoothness, heat, noise, runtime.

2

Hibernate instead of Modern Standby (Phase 4). Windows never really sleeps. This is why the handheld is flat when you pull it out of a bag, and it's a ten-minute fix.

3

Four power profiles, not three (Phase 5). Stock Armoury Crate has no concept of "docked, fan noise is irrelevant" or "on a train at 40 %".

01

Baseline

~30 minNo riskUSB drive

Skipping this doesn't break anything — it just means you'll never know whether the rest worked, or which change to undo when something regresses.

Record what you're starting from

PowerShell
Get-ComputerInfo -Property OsName,OsVersion,OsBuildNumber,BiosSMBIOSBIOSVersion
Get-CimInstance Win32_VideoController | Select-Object Name,DriverVersion,DriverDate

Also note your BIOS/MCU versions and Armoury Crate SE version from Update Center.

Benchmark

Pick something repeatable — a built-in benchmark (Cyberpunk 2077, Shadow of the Tomb Raider) has low run-to-run variance. Consistency matters far more than which one. Run at Turbo, plugged in, 1080p native, upscaling off — you're measuring hardware, not FSR. Record average FPS, 1 % lows, temperatures, package power.

Battery — two numbers

Active: 30 minutes of play at Performance mode. Note the percentage lost and the wattage from the overlay.

Standby: charge up, one press of the power button, leave it 8+ hours untouched, check it in the morning. Write the number down — Phase 4 exists to fix it.

PowerShell — reports worth keeping
powercfg /batteryreport /output "$HOME\Desktop\battery-baseline.html"
powercfg /sleepstudy   /output "$HOME\Desktop\sleepstudy-baseline.html"
powercfg /a   # which sleep states exist — want Hibernate listed
Insurance

Save your BitLocker recovery key to your Microsoft account and somewhere physical, and build ASUS recovery media on a 16 GB USB stick. You almost certainly won't need either. The cost of not having them when you do is the whole drive.

02

Firmware and drivers

~45 minLow riskStay plugged in

Stale firmware is the most common cause of "loud fan, bad battery." ASUS shipped real power and thermal fixes across this device's life. Get current before tuning, or you'll be tuning around bugs that were already fixed.

Caution

Keep it on AC for this entire phase. A BIOS or MCU flash interrupted by a flat battery is the one way to genuinely brick the handheld.

In Armoury Crate SE → Settings → Update Center (check MyASUS too — they occasionally surface different packages), install in this order, rebooting when asked: MCU firmware → BIOS → chipset/control-interface packages. Re-check afterward; updates chain and one round often isn't enough.

Armoury Crate SE updates separately from firmware — people miss this constantly.

Graphics driver — a real decision

OptionGets youCosts you
ASUS-validated
start here		 Tuned for this power envelope, validated against your firmware, fewer power-state oddities Lags AMD's public releases, sometimes by months
Generic AMD
Adrenalin		 Newer FSR runtime, access to AFMF 2 frame generation, day-one fixes Occasionally regresses handheld power behavior; ASUS doesn't validate it

Do Phases 3–11 on the ASUS build, then try Adrenalin and measure whether it helped you specifically. Switch with DDU — never install one over the other. Download both the tool and your target driver first, disconnect Wi-Fi so Windows doesn't race you, clean and restart, then install.

Watch for

Windows Update sometimes silently replaces your graphics driver with an older version. If frame rate mysteriously drops a week from now, check the driver version first — it's usually this.

03

Windows tuning

~1 hrLow riskAll reversible

Deliberately not a debloat script. Those break things in ways that surface three weeks later with no obvious cause. Every item says what it buys you, so you can skip what you don't want.

Choose a shell

The desktop is the wrong front end for a 7″ touchscreen held in two hands. Two good options:

  • Xbox Full Screen Experience — Microsoft's controller-first shell, now available on all Windows 11 handhelds rather than just the Xbox Ally. Aggregates Steam and Game Pass, and suspends much of the desktop shell, freeing memory and background cycles. Settings → Gaming → Full screen experience.
  • Steam Big Picture at startup — simpler, fewer layers, good if everything you play is on Steam anyway.

Recommendation: FSE as the shell, Steam inside it. You get the overhead reduction either way, and FSE handles the non-Steam launchers you'll inevitably accumulate.

Disable VBS / Memory Integrity

Consistently the largest single frame-rate recovery on Z1 Extreme handhelds — the hypervisor layer costs real performance on a low-power APU. Windows Security → Device security → Core isolation → Memory integrity → Off, then reboot. Verify in msinfo32.

The trade-off, stated plainly

Memory Integrity blocks a genuine class of driver-based attacks. Turning it off measurably lowers your defenses. On a gaming-only handheld that's reasonable; on a device where you do banking and work email, less so. Two clicks to reverse either way.

The rest

  • Power mode → Balanced. Not "Best power efficiency," which throttles the CPU even on AC and makes people think their device is defective.
  • Prune startup apps. Where handheld idle drain genuinely originates — every launcher you've ever installed wants to start with Windows. Keep Armoury Crate and ASUS services; drop Epic, EA, Ubisoft, Battle.net, GOG, Spotify, OneDrive.
  • HAGS on, VRR on, Game Mode on. VRR is required for Phase 7 to work.
  • Background capture off — costs frames continuously for a feature most people never use.
  • Do not disturb on, search indexing set to Classic rather than Enhanced.

Don't do these

  • Third-party debloat scripts — they strip components Windows Update later expects, and breakage surfaces weeks later with no traceable cause.
  • Disabling Defender — small cost on Zen 4, and you just reduced your security posture above.
  • Disabling SysMain or the page file — widely recommended, and both make things worse on a 24 GB machine that then stutters when a game wants memory you told Windows not to reserve.
  • Manual CPU affinity or priority per game — the scheduler does it better, and it resets on reboot anyway.

04

Sleep, standby, and battery life

~30 minLow risk+ overnight test
Why this phase matters most

A SteamOS-style handheld suspends and resumes like a Switch. Windows uses Modern Standby, where the machine never truly sleeps — it keeps the network alive and lets background tasks wake it. Leave the Ally X "asleep" in a bag overnight and you can find it badly drained, or hot, or flat. Almost every guide skips this.

The fix: hibernate for long sleeps, standby for short ones.

PowerShell (Administrator) — enable hibernate
powercfg /a      # confirm Hibernate is listed
powercfg /h on   # enable it if not

Make the power button hibernate

The single highest-value change here. One press should put the device away properly.

PowerShell (Administrator)
# 0=nothing 1=sleep 2=hibernate 3=shutdown 4=display off
powercfg /setdcvalueindex SCHEME_CURRENT SUB_BUTTONS PBUTTONACTION 2
powercfg /setacvalueindex SCHEME_CURRENT SUB_BUTTONS PBUTTONACTION 2
powercfg /setactive SCHEME_CURRENT

The power button is also your fingerprint reader — hibernate resume still prompts normally.

Hibernate automatically after a short sleep

Best of both: instant resume for a bathroom break, proper shutdown before it goes in a bag.

PowerShell (Administrator) — seconds
# 30 min on battery, 60 min plugged in
powercfg /setdcvalueindex SCHEME_CURRENT SUB_SLEEP HIBERNATEIDLE 1800
powercfg /setacvalueindex SCHEME_CURRENT SUB_SLEEP HIBERNATEIDLE 3600
powercfg /setactive SCHEME_CURRENT

Stop things waking it

Run powercfg /lastwake and powercfg /devicequery wake_armed to find the culprit — usually the Wi-Fi adapter. Disarm it in Device Manager → Power Management, then disable wake timers in advanced power settings.

Cap the charge at 80 %

You dock regularly, so the device sits at 100 % on AC — the main long-term degradation path for a lithium cell. MyASUS → Battery Care Mode, set to 80 %. Costs roughly a fifth of your runtime, meaningfully slows capacity loss. Lift it before a trip. If you're mostly away from power, this is the wrong trade — use the full charge and accept the wear.

Expected runtime · 80 Wh, under game load

Why these look low

TDP is the APU's budget, not the system's — display, fans, SSD, and radios add roughly 5–8 W on top. A "15 W" profile really pulls 21–23 W, which is why 80 Wh ÷ 15 W = 5.3 hours on paper but ~3.5 in practice. Figures quoting 5–6 hours at 15 W are measuring light load, not gaming.

ProfileTDPRuntimeTypical use
Battery Sipper10–13 W4–6 hr2D, indie, emulation, older titles
Handheld Default15–17 W3–3.5 hrMost AAA at 900p + FSR
Handheld Max25 W2–2.5 hrDemanding titles, short sessions

At an 80 % cap, subtract about a fifth. The biggest runtime lever isn't TDP — it's capping frame rate (Phase 7).

05

Armoury Crate power profiles

~45 minNo riskGUI only

Stock gives you Silent / Performance / Turbo — three answers to a question that has four. It has no idea whether you're on a couch with an outlet or on a train at 40 %, and no concept of "docked, fan noise is irrelevant."

ProfileTDPFanWhen
Battery Sipper10–13 WQuiet2D, indie, emulation. Maximum runtime
Handheld Default15–17 WBalancedYour everyday driver — best perf-per-watt
Handheld Max25 WPerformancePlugged in on the couch, demanding titles
Docked30 WAggressiveExternal display, noise doesn't matter

Why 15–17 W is the sweet spot

The Z1 Extreme's efficiency curve flattens noticeably past roughly 17 W. Going 15 → 25 W costs about 60 % more power for 15–20 % more frame rate in GPU-limited titles — and that's the good case. Below ~13 W the GPU starves and frame pacing gets choppy even when the average looks fine.

Set all three power limits, not just one

They differ in how long each limit applies, not just how high:

ValueApplies forSlider range
SPLIndefinitely — the real ceiling7–30 W
sPPT~30 s to a couple of minutes15–43 W
fPPT~5–10 s bursts15–53 W
Note the floors

sPPT and fPPT cannot go below 15 W, whatever SPL is. At SPL 10 W you can't set a boost limit "2 W above" — leave both at 15 W and let the floor do its job.

ProfileSPLsPPTfPPT
Battery Sipper10–131515
Handheld Default172022
Handheld Max252830
Docked303335

Setting all three equal where you can flattens boost response and makes the device feel sluggish in menus and during loading. 30 W is plugged-in only — on battery the ceiling is 25 W, so the Docked profile silently behaves as Handheld Max if you undock without switching.

Fan curves

The stock curve is conservative until it abruptly isn't: it stays quiet, lets temperature climb, then ramps hard. The audible change is more annoying than steady noise, so ramp earlier and more gradually. You can't damage anything — the APU throttles rather than cooks.

Common self-inflicted problem

If a profile ever feels slower than it should, check temperatures before anything else. A too-quiet fan curve causes throttling that reads as a mysterious performance loss.

VRAM

Auto is correct for the large majority of titles — with 24 GB the driver has room to manage it. Force 6 GB only when you hit a specific symptom (texture-load stutter, or a game refusing to launch over video memory), 8 GB if that didn't fix it. Don't over-allocate "just in case": fixed VRAM is carved permanently out of system memory and starves the game of the RAM it also needs.

Bind Operating Mode, the performance overlay, and the FPS limiter to Command Center. Leave the rear buttons unmapped here — Steam Input handles them better per-game in Phase 6.

Steam setup

~45 minNo risk

Most of the value here is Steam Input — the most underused feature on Windows handhelds.

If you're using FSE as your shell, set Steam to start minimized so you don't get two full-screen UIs fighting on boot. Your Steam library appears in FSE automatically.

Rear buttons (M1 / M2)

Reachable without lifting your thumbs off the sticks, which is exactly why they're worth mapping per-game rather than globally.

ShootersCrouch / slideMelee or grenade

RacingLook behindHandbrake
Anything with menusMapQuick save

Gyro aiming

The Ally X has a gyroscope and most people never turn it on. Used as fine aim on top of the right stick — not as a replacement — it meaningfully improves precision.

  • Behavior: As Mouse
  • Activation: On Right Stick Touch — so the device doesn't fight you when you shift grip
  • Sensitivity: start low and raise it. Too high is unusable and puts people off the feature permanently

Give it a couple of hours. It feels wrong for twenty minutes and then feels obvious.

Since you play both handheld and docked, build two controller configurations for anything you play in both contexts — different controller, different ergonomics, different stick curves, and you probably don't want gyro on a couch pad.

Launch options worth knowing

OptionEffect
-vulkan / -dx12Force a renderer. On RDNA 3, Vulkan or DX12 is usually faster where a game offers both — test per title
-fullscreenExclusive fullscreen, avoiding compositor overhead
-novidSkips intro videos (Source games and others)

Don't paste launch options you found online without knowing what they do — many are cargo-culted from desktop hardware and several are counterproductive on an APU.

Turn on shader pre-caching (do the background processing plugged in — it's a real power draw), set downloads to throttle while games are running, and consider only update when I launch so the device doesn't wake to download 40 GB you didn't ask for.

07

Display and upscaling

~30 minNo riskBiggest lever
Read this one

No registry tweak, driver swap, or TDP profile comes close to the effect of choosing a sensible render resolution and frame target. The Z1 Extreme is a 12-CU GPU at 15–30 W; asking it for native 1080p Ultra is asking the wrong question. Render low, upscale smart, cap the frame rate.

Cap your frame rate

Counterintuitive and the most impactful battery setting on the device: a capped 40 fps often beats an uncapped 55 — smoother pacing, lower draw, longer session, less noise. With VRR on a 120 Hz panel these are all clean: 30 for heavy AAA, 40 as the handheld sweet spot, 60 where responsiveness matters, 90–120 for 2D and competitive titles.

Settings to cut first

Expensive and least visible at 7″, in order: shadows (usually the single most expensive setting — medium is nearly always enough), ray tracing (off, whatever the menu implies), volumetric fog, ambient occlusion, heavy anti-aliasing stacked on top of upscaling. Keep textures high — you have memory to spare and it's the setting you actually see.

Which upscaler

  • In-game FSR — your default. It has motion vectors and depth data, so it beats driver-level scaling. Quality preset; Balanced when you need more.
  • RSR — the fallback. Driver-level, works with any game including ones with no FSR support. Lower quality because it's working on the final image blind. Use only where FSR isn't offered.
  • AFMF 2 — frame generation. Worth it for single-player, slower titles already at 40+ fps. Not for competitive or twitchy games, and not below ~40 fps where artifacts get obvious. It adds input latency — it makes a game look smoother while feeling the same or slightly worse.

In AMD Software: Radeon Image Sharpening on (~70–80 %, nearly free, offsets upscaling softness) and Anti-Lag on. Skip Radeon Boost — the resolution flickering during motion is distracting at handheld distances.

Docked: output 1080p, not 4K

The Z1 Extreme has no business rendering 4K, and your TV's scaler is better at upscaling 1080p than the GPU is at rendering a quarter-rate 4K image. Exceptions: 2D, older titles, emulation, and anything already comfortably above 60 fps.

08

Docked mode

~1 hrNo riskHardware choices

Mostly about three unglamorous choices — which port, which cable, which charger — which are exactly the three most people get wrong.

Use the USB4 port

The two USB-C ports are not equivalent. USB4 has full DisplayPort alt mode, the highest bandwidth, and the best PD negotiation; the USB 3.2 Gen 2 port is more limited when driving display, power, and peripherals simultaneously. USB4 is what replaced the original Ally's proprietary XG Mobile connector, which is why it's the capable one.

The cable is not a detail

Use a certified Ultra High Speed HDMI (2.1) cable. The cheap one in your drawer is the most common reason a dock "won't do 120 Hz" — it'll happily negotiate 60 Hz and silently refuse more, with no error explaining why.

The 65 W problem

Docked, the Ally X runs a ~30 W profile, charges an 80 Wh battery, and powers everything in the dock. The included 65 W charger can't always cover it. Symptoms are confusing rather than obvious: the battery slowly drains while "charging," or performance quietly drops. Fix: a 100 W USB-C PD GaN charger. Check your dock's passthrough rating too — some reserve 15–20 W for themselves.

Dock requirements in priority order: HDMI 2.1, 100 W PD passthrough, Gigabit Ethernet (kills wireless latency, transforms 100 GB installs), USB-A ports, and a cradle that doesn't block the top exhaust vent.

On the Windows side: set the TV as the only display rather than running both panels, set 1080p at the highest refresh the chain supports, and verify it actually negotiated in Advanced display settings. Audio doesn't always follow the display — set it separately. Then switch to the Docked profile from Phase 5.

09

Storage and library

~30 minNo risk
Good news specific to your device

The original ROG Ally had a notorious problem: its microSD reader sat beside the exhaust vent and sustained heat killed cards. The Ally X relocated the reader. Most advice warning you off microSD predates your hardware.

Get a UHS-II card — the reader supports it, and it's roughly double the sequential read of UHS-I in practice. 512 GB or 1 TB, V60/V90, from a retailer you trust. Verify it with H2testw or F3 when it arrives — counterfeits report the right capacity and corrupt data past their real size, and you'd rather know before installing 200 GB.

TierWhat goes here
Internal NVMeCurrent main game, open-world titles that stream assets continuously, competitive games where load times matter
microSDBacklog, indie, emulation, 2D, older titles, anything you play occasionally

Rule of thumb: if it stutters when you move fast through a big world, it belongs on the NVMe.

Add the card as a Steam library folder, and use Steam → Settings → Storage → Move to relocate installed games without re-downloading. Never move game folders in Explorer — Steam loses track and forces a full re-download. Keep at least 10 % of the internal drive free; NVMe drives slow measurably when nearly full.

Consider

A controller for docked play (Xbox pads just work; DualSense works well with Steam Input). A cooling stand if you play long docked sessions in a warm room — redundant if your dock has a fan, and not worth the bulk handheld. A 65 W+ power bank for travel; note the 80 Wh internal battery means a 20,000 mAh bank is roughly one extra charge, and check airline limits.

Skip

  • "Gaming" microSD cards at a premium — UHS-II is what matters, not branding
  • Anything advertising 8K on a device that shouldn't render 4K
  • Thermal pads and paste — outside your constraints, and stock cooling is competent
  • Privacy filters — they cut brightness on a panel that needs all of it outdoors
  • Docks without PD passthrough, and cheap unbranded hubs (where flaky dock behavior lives)
If you only buy two things

The 100 W charger and the HDMI 2.1 cable. Together they cost very little and eliminate the two most common docked problems — problems that otherwise present as vague performance complaints you'd spend an evening chasing through software.

11

Validation

~1 hrNo risk+ overnight test

Everything above is a claim until you measure it. This is what turns the guide from "things I read on the internet" into "things I verified on my device" — and tells you which changes actually did something for your library.

Repeat the Phase 1 benchmark under identical conditions. 1 % lows are the number that matters most — average FPS is what marketing quotes; 1 % lows are what you feel. Frame pacing gains from fan curves and drivers show up here while barely moving the average.

Be honest with yourself

The Windows-side changes are worth a modest but real improvement, not a transformation. If you see a dramatic jump, suspect your methodology before you celebrate — check both runs used the same preset and that the first wasn't thermally throttled.

Then the battery comparison that usually changes how people play: same game, 30 minutes on Handheld Default, versus 30 minutes capped at 40 fps with FSR Quality at 900p.

And the standby test — the measurement nobody does. With hibernate configured, the overnight drop should be essentially zero against whatever you recorded in Phase 1.

If something got worse

Work backward, most likely first: driver swap (roll back with DDU) → fan curve too quiet causing throttling → a per-game profile overriding the one you think is active → VRAM forced when Auto was fine → frame generation enabled where it doesn't belong.
